eyebrow: Preserving the nuclear testing moratorium
title: What Counts as a Prohibited Nuclear Explosion?
paragraph: Recent concerns regarding China’s alleged covert nuclear test in 2020 have revived old debates about the effectiveness of the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty’s (CTBT) verification regime in detecting low-yield nuclear tests, as well as concerns about the fragility of the norm against testing amid broader developments. Ambiguities around definitions, and what the treaty text permits and prohibits, have complicated the situation further. As the CTBT marks thirty years since it opened for signature, the treaty has still not entered into force, and therefore, its consultations and clarifications and on-site inspection mechanisms remain unavailable. In the current strained geopolitical environment, the question of what can be done to preserve the nuclear testing moratorium is therefore an urgent one, particularly in the absence of the CTBT’s entry-into-force and of additional voluntary confidence-building measures, at a time when the risk of nuclear experiments or activities that may be prohibited being conducted and going undetected, or being misinterpreted, is rising.

paragraph: Article I of the CTBT states that each State Party "undertakes not to carry out any nuclear weapon test explosion or any other nuclear explosion, and to prohibit and prevent any such nuclear explosion at any place under its jurisdiction or control." At what point does permitted experiment become a prohibited nuclear explosion? Explore these considerations below.

# ---------- KEY TERMS (the fold-out list under the scale) ----------

TERM
name: Zero yield
text: The US definition prohibits all nuclear explosions that produce a self-sustaining chain reaction of any kind. Earlier definitions required that fission energy be less than the chemical energy released by the high explosive. Even by the US reading, zero yield does not mean zero nuclear yield.

TERM
name: Subcritical experiment
text: An experiment that does not produce a self-sustaining fission chain reaction. The yield of fission energy is minuscule, comparable to that from the explosion of 0.1 milligrams of TNT.

TERM
name: Hydrodynamic and hydronuclear experiments
text: Hydrodynamic experiments involve special nuclear material but no self-sustaining nuclear reaction, and are treated as legal under the CTBT. Hydronuclear experiments cross the self-sustaining threshold, and are prohibited under the US reading. Russia has never accepted the US definition of zero yield.

TERM
name: Decoupling
text: Detonating a nuclear device within a large, excavated cavity to dampen the resulting seismic signals. The Sterling test of 1966 is reported as the only one to come close to full decoupling, with a signal about 70 times smaller than a normally coupled test.

TERM
name: The International Monitoring System (IMS)
text: The CTBTO's global network of 308 certified facilities. It was originally designed to detect tests of about 1,000 tonnes of TNT equivalent, and today its detection goes down to 500 tonnes. On-site inspections, the treaty's final verification measure, only become available once the treaty enters into force.

TERM
name: How the readout is calculated
text: "What the seismometers see" is the real yield divided by the decoupling factor you choose (10, 20 or 70), and the real yield when decoupling is off. It is compared with the IMS level you choose. The Hiroshima comparison divides your yield by 15 kt. On the ruler, a yield's position is ((log10 of the yield in kg + 7.5) / 18.6) squared, which spreads large yields further apart than a plain log scale would.

QUIZ INTRO
heading: How is each event under the CTBT widely understood?
intro: Pick how each event is widely understood under the treaty: permitted, prohibited or unclear, and guess whether some statements are true or false. This should give you an idea of the several of ambiguities, technicalities and differing interpretations around what constitutes a prohibited nuclear explosion. Don't worry if you are unsure – that really is the point!

QUESTION
question: Subcritical experiments with fissile material
answer: permitted
explain: Subcritical experiments, which do not produce a self-sustaining fission chain reaction, are permitted. Although, it is worth keeping in mind that some non-nuclear weapon states under the NPT view subcritical experiments as a violation of the spirit, if not letter of the CTBT.

QUESTION
question: Hydronuclear tests releasing a few kilograms of TNT equivalent
answer: unclear or prohibited
explain if unclear: Depends on whom you ask. The US definition of zero yield prohibits all nuclear explosions that produce a self-sustaining supercritical chain reaction of any kind. However, Russia reportedly considered that the release of nuclear energy from permitted experiments might range up to 100 kg of TNT equivalent, unlike the US limit of around 2 kg. Reported declarations from Russian officials indicate that testing with yields around 100 kg is not a violation of the hydronuclear regime. Despite differing interpretations, hydronuclear tests are widely regarded as prohibited under the CTBT.
explain if prohibited: Under the US definition of zero yield, to which the CTBT is commonly referred, all nuclear explosions that produce a self-sustaining supercritical chain reaction of any kind are prohibited. While it is worth bearing in mind that Russia reportedly considers tests involving yields of around 100 kg to be permissible, hydronuclear tests are widely regarded as prohibited under the CTBT.
explain if permitted: Under the US definition of zero yield, to which the CTBT is commonly referred, all nuclear explosions that produce a self-sustaining supercritical chain reaction of any kind are prohibited. While it is worth noting that reported declarations from Russian officials suggest that testing with yields of around 100 kg would not constitute a prohibited explosion, hydronuclear tests are widely regarded as prohibited under the CTBT.
show verdict: no

QUESTION
question: Fully-contained hydronuclear tests conducted inside a containment vessel in a laboratory
answer: unclear or prohibited
explain: Again, it depends on whom you ask. According to the US interpretation of the zero-yield standard, hydronuclear tests are prohibited, regardless of whether they are conducted in a laboratory containment vessel. However, it is unlikely that Russia considers hydronuclear 'experiments' (or tests) to be prohibited 'explosions'. Despite certain reported divergences, hydronuclear tests are widely regarded as prohibited under the CTBT.
show verdict: no

QUESTION
question: Nuclear tests set off in an underground salt cavity to muffle/reduce the seismic signal
answer: prohibited
explain: A nuclear test in a salt cavity to muffle or reduce the seismic signal would be a cheating scenario to evade monitoring capabilities. Known as a "decoupled explosion," these explosions are therefore as prohibited as any other.

QUESTION
question: Peaceful nuclear explosions conducted for non-military purposes such as the large-scale excavation to create reservoirs, canals and ports
answer: prohibited
explain: The CTBT bans all nuclear explosions, whether for military or peaceful purposes.

QUESTION
question: Computer-based modelling or simulations to test weapon designs
answer: permitted
explain: Computer simulations to replace nuclear materials are widely regarded as permitted under the CTBT. In the Clinton administration's article-by-article analysis of the CTBT, computer modelling was recognised as an activity not affected by the treaty in order to ensure the safety and reliability of its nuclear weapons stockpile.

QUESTION
question: Inertial confinement fusion experiments
answer: permitted
explain: The Clinton administration's article-by-article analysis of the treaty lists "activities not prohibited", which includes: hydrodynamic experiments that might involve sub-critical amounts of fissile materials, inertial confinement fusion (ICF) experiments, experiments using pulsed-power facilities and the operation of nuclear power or research reactors.

QUESTION
question: A 10 kt underground nuclear test, well below the TTBT threshold of 150 kt
answer: prohibited
explain: The 1974 TTBT permitted underground tests up to 150 kt, but underground nuclear testing is prohibited by the CTBT.

QUESTION
question: Non-nuclear chemical high-explosive experiments in order to better detect low-yield nuclear explosions
answer: permitted
explain: It is not a nuclear explosion. In September 2026, the NNSA conducted one such chemical high-explosive experiment at the Nevada National Security Site to improve detection of decoupled explosions.

QUESTION
question: True or false: 
Hydrodynamic tests that do not produce nuclear yield are prohibited to the non-nuclear-weapon states of the NPT
answer: true
explain: These tests are prohibited to non-nuclear-weapon states by the NPT because they relate to a weapons program, which these states are prohibited from conducting.

QUESTION
question: True or false: 
All nuclear tests that cannot be detected by the CTBTO's IMS network are permitted by default, given the perceived low military or strategic value
answer: false
explain: Whether a test can be detected or not has no bearing on whether it is prohibited. A hydronuclear test may fall below what the IMS can detect, yet it is widely considered to be prohibited under the treaty. Decoupled nuclear explosions are prohibited despite the challenges in detecting and identifying such events.

QUESTION
question: True or false: 
The CTBT establishes a yield threshold that defines a nuclear explosion that would be prohibited under the treaty. 
answer: false
explain: Unlike the 150-kt limit in the TTBT, the CTBT does not establish a yield threshold that defines a nuclear explosion that would be prohibited under the treaty.
